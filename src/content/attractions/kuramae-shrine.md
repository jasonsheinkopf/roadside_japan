A modest neighborhood shrine one minute from Kuramae Station, founded in 1693 by the fifth Tokugawa shogun — and quietly one of the more historically loaded small shrines in Tokyo.

## Why It's Interesting

During the Edo period, the shogunate's rice storehouses lined this bank of the Sumida River, giving the district its name (Kuramae = "in front of the storehouse"). The shrine itself was revered as a guardian of Edo Castle's northeast "demon gate" — but its more surprising legacy is sumo: kanjin-zumo, fundraising tournaments held on the shrine grounds to finance shrines and temples, are considered the direct ancestor of modern professional sumo. A memorial stele to the wrestlers who competed here still stands on the grounds.

## Best Time to Visit

Early March brings cherry blossoms and mimosa blooming together, a combination the shrine is locally known for — but it's a worthwhile five-minute stop any time of year.

## Getting There

One minute from Kuramae Station's A6 exit, directly on the walk between Ueno and Asakusabashi/Ryogoku.
