On a vineyard-covered ridge above the wine town of **Weinsberg**, in Baden-Württemberg's Heilbronn district, stands **Burgruine Weibertreu** — a castle ruin whose name literally means "wifely loyalty," earned by one of medieval Germany's most-repeated legends.

## Why It's Interesting

The castle, originally Burg Weinsberg, probably dates to the early 11th century and sits atop the 272-meter Burgberg overlooking the town. Its fame comes from a single day: **December 21, 1140**, when King Conrad III besieged Weinsberg during his conflict with the House of Welf. When the town surrendered, the king allowed the women inside to leave freely, carrying only what they could bear on their own backs. Rather than take valuables, every wife carried her own husband out of the gates. Conrad III, the story goes, laughed and honored his word rather than punish the trick — and the castle has been known as **Weibertreu** ("wifely loyalty") ever since. The tale was popular enough to be recorded by the Brothers Grimm and retold across centuries of German folklore.

The castle itself didn't survive as well as the legend: it was destroyed during the **German Peasants' War of 1525** and never rebuilt as a fortress. What remains today is a preserved ruin — walls, a tower used as a viewpoint, and a chapel — reached by a footpath through the vineyards that now cover most of the hill, with a small café serving homemade cake at the top.

## Best Time to Visit

The ruin is open year-round (closing only for ice or snow), but a clear afternoon in late spring through autumn is best — the vineyard slopes are green or turning color, and the valley view from the tower is at its widest.

## Getting There

Burgruine Weibertreu sits above Weinsberg at **Burgweg, 74189 Weinsberg**, a short regional-rail hop from Heilbronn. There's no parking at the ruin itself — park in town or at a lot near the base of the hill, then follow the footpath up through the vineyards.
