Tucked a few minutes from the sumo stadium, Ekoin is easy to walk past — and packed with more layered history than almost anywhere else in Ryogoku.

## Why It's Interesting

The temple was founded in 1657 in the aftermath of the Great Fire of Meireki, which destroyed 60-70% of Edo and killed roughly 100,000 people, most without surviving family to bury them. A century later, in 1768, the temple grounds became a home for kanjin-zumo — charity sumo tournaments licensed by the shogunate — and by 1833 it was hosting every official sumo tournament in the country, a run that lasted 76 years until the sport moved next door into the first Ryogoku Kokugikan. The 1937 Chikara-zuka monument still draws young sumo apprentices praying for strength. And in one of Tokyo's odder pilgrimage spots, the grave of Nezumi Kozo — a chivalrous Edo-period thief who stole from the wealthy — has been worn into a nub by generations of students chipping off stone shavings for exam-luck charms.

## Best Time to Visit

Any time — it's outdoors, free, and rarely crowded, a quiet counterpoint to the sumo stadium's bustle nearby.

## Getting There

About five minutes on foot from Ryogoku Station, right alongside the cluster of sumo-related sights in the neighborhood.
