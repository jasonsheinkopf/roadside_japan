**Onioshidashi Park** (鬼押出し園, *Onioshidashi-en*) preserves one of the most dramatic lava fields in Japan: a stretch of rugged black rock, roughly 5km long, left behind when Mt. Asama's catastrophic 1783 eruption sent lava and pyroclastic material pouring across the landscape and cooling in place. Walking paths wind between the formations to a hillside temple with views back over the whole field.

## Why It's Interesting

The name translates roughly to "where the demons pushed the rock out" — the eruption was violent enough that witnesses at the time described it in those terms, and the landscape still looks the part: block-like, jagged lava piled across more than a thousand hectares, with Mt. Asama itself rising behind it. At the center of the park stands **Asama Kannon-dō**, a branch temple of Tokyo's Kan'ei-ji, built in 1958 to honor those who died in the eruption — a quiet, deliberate counterpoint to the raw geology around it.

## Best Time to Visit

The park is open year-round (8:00–17:00, last admission 16:30). From mid-May, alpine flowers — Iwakagami, Tsugazakura, and other high-altitude species — bloom directly out of the cooled lava, and autumn brings clearer long-distance views of Mt. Asama and Mt. Shirane from the temple's overlook.

## Getting There

There's no direct train or bus stop, so a car is effectively required. The park sits at around 1,300m elevation in Tsumagoi Village, roughly 20 minutes from Kusatsu Onsen and 30–40 minutes from Karuizawa — both make it a natural stopping point on the drive from Shiga Kōgen back toward Tokyo via the Joshin'etsu Expressway.
