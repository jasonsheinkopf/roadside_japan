The building that anchors the entire neighborhood — a 10,000-plus-seat arena that's hosted Japan's national sport since 1985, standing on ground that's been sumo territory since long before that.

## Why It's Interesting

Three times a year — January, May, September — the Kokugikan fills for a 15-day grand tournament (honbasho), the closest thing sumo has to a Super Bowl. Outside those weeks, the building holds a museum with around 30,000 items pulled from sumo's history: woodblock prints of legendary wrestlers, embroidered ceremonial aprons, championship trophies and banners. It's a small museum, but a dense one, and it's the rare Tokyo museum that costs nothing at all to see.

## Best Time to Visit

Here's the catch worth planning around: the museum keeps weekday hours (10:00-16:30) and is closed on Saturdays and Sundays outside tournament weeks — so on a Saturday-morning walk, the museum itself will likely be shut. The stadium and its surrounding streets are still worth the detour regardless; if the timing ever lines up with a January, May, or September tournament, it's worth restructuring the whole day around it.

## Getting There

One minute from Ryogoku Station's JR Sobu Line exit — you'll see it before you arrive.
