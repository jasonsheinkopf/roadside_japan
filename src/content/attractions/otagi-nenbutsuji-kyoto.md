Up a wooded hill past Arashiyama's bamboo grove, well outside the crowds that stop at the more famous temples, **Otagi Nenbutsu-ji** covers its hillside grounds with **1,200 stone rakan statues** — and every one of them looks completely different, because ordinary volunteers, not professional sculptors, carved them.

## Why It's Interesting

The temple itself dates back centuries — traditionally founded under Empress Shōtoku and revived in 911 by the monk Senkan before relocating to its current Sagano hillside site in 1922 — but its real draw arrived between **1981 and 1991**, when the head priest invited the public to carve their own stone rakan (disciples of the Buddha) for the grounds. What began as a planned 500 statues grew to **1,200** once word spread and requests poured in, and the result is unlike any other statue garden in Kyoto: monks caught mid-laugh, mid-nap, holding sake cups, wearing sunglasses, playing instruments — each one an amateur's personal, unsupervised idea of what a stone monk could look like. Decades of moss and weather have only made them stranger and softer. The main hall, a mid-Kamakura-period structure, is separately designated an Important Cultural Property.

## Best Time to Visit

Open year-round; a light dusting of autumn color in November plays nicely against the grey, moss-covered stone, but the statues themselves are the reason to visit in any season. Skip Wednesdays and Saturdays, when the temple is closed.

## Getting There

From **Arashiyama Station**, Kyoto Bus route 94 (bound for Kiyotaki) takes about 15 minutes to the Atagoji-mae stop directly outside; on foot from Saga-Arashiyama, it's a hilly 40–45 minute walk past the bamboo grove and Adashino Nenbutsu-ji. About 10 free parking spaces are available on site for those driving.
