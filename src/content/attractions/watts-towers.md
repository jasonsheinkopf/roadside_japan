## One man, no blueprint, thirty-three years

Sabato "Simon" Rodia, an Italian immigrant construction worker and tile setter, spent from 1921 to 1954 building a cluster of 17 interconnected towers in his backyard — alone, with no scaffolding, engineering degree, or master plan. He called the work *Nuestro Pueblo* ("our town"). The tallest spire reaches about 99.5 feet, held together by steel rebar wrapped in wire mesh and hand-troweled mortar, studded with a mosaic of broken tile, glass bottles, seashells, and pottery shards he collected around the neighborhood.

In 1955, worn down by permit fights with the City of Los Angeles, Rodia deeded the property to a neighbor and left Watts for good, never to see the towers again. Two years later, actor Nicholas King and film editor William Cartwright bought the lot for $2,000 to save the towers from demolition. They're now a National Historic Landmark, a California Historical Landmark, and listed on the National Register of Historic Places; a Los Angeles County Museum of Art-led restoration reopened the site to visitors in November 2022.

## Getting There

The towers sit in the Watts neighborhood of South Los Angeles, directly across the street from the 103rd St/Watts Towers Metro Blue Line station — genuinely one of the easiest transit-accessible oddities in the city. The site itself is outdoor and free to view any time from the sidewalk; the adjacent Watts Towers Arts Center runs ticketed guided tours on a limited weekly schedule.

## Best Time to Visit

Go on a clear day — the mosaic glass catches the light best in late-morning or afternoon sun. Check the Arts Center's current tour schedule before visiting if you want to get inside the fenced perimeter for a closer look.
