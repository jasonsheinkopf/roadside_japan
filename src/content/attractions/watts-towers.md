## One man, thirty-three years

Simon Rodia, an Italian immigrant construction worker, bought a wedge-shaped lot in Watts in 1921 and started building. For the next 33 years — working alone, after hours, with hand tools, a window-washer's belt, and no plans — he raised seventeen interconnected structures of steel rebar wrapped in wire mesh and hand-packed mortar, the tallest spire reaching nearly 100 feet. He never welded or bolted a single joint.

## Trash into treasure

Every reachable surface is a mosaic of found material: green 7-Up bottles, blue Milk of Magnesia glass, seashells from nearby beaches, broken tiles and pottery donated by neighborhood kids for pennies. Rodia set them all in patterns that catch the California sun. He called the work *Nuestro Pueblo* — "our town."

## The stress test

In 1954, Rodia deeded the property to a neighbor and left Watts, never to return. When the city declared the towers unsafe in 1959 and moved to demolish them, preservationists negotiated a load test: a crane pulled on the tallest tower with 10,000 pounds of force. The tower didn't budge — the test equipment failed instead. The towers were saved, and are now a National Historic Landmark, a California Historical Landmark, and one of the most important works of vernacular/outsider architecture in the world.

## Visiting

The towers stand at 1727 E 107th Street in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles, beside the Watts Towers Arts Center. The exterior is visible anytime; guided tours (typically Thursday–Sunday) take you inside the enclosure. The Metro A Line's 103rd Street/Watts Towers station is a short walk away.
