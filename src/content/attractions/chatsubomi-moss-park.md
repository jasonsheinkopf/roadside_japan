**Chatsubomi Moss Park** (チャツボミゴケ公園, *Chatsubomigoke Kōen*) protects one of the strangest wetlands in Japan: a stream bed carpeted in vivid, electric-green moss that thrives in water acidic enough to sting. The moss grows out of **Anajigoku** ("the hell pit"), a spring born of Mt. Shirane's volcanic activity, on the site of a former iron mine deep in the mountains above Nakanojo, Gunma.

## Why It's Interesting

Chatsubomi moss is the most acid-tolerant of the roughly 1,800 known moss species on Earth, and this is essentially the only place on Honshu where it grows in the wild. The spring water runs at a pH of roughly 2.6–2.9 — strong enough that iron bacteria and the moss itself precipitate iron ore out of it, a process that once made this a working mine (Gunma Iron Mine, closed in 1965 when the deposit ran out). In 2017, the government designated the site — the spring, the moss colony, and the iron-forming riverbed together — a national Natural Monument. What's left today is a short, otherworldly walk along a stream that looks less like Japan and more like a nature documentary about extremophiles.

## Best Time to Visit

The park's reception is open mid-April through November only, closed entirely for winter. Fresh green in the early-summer months makes a striking contrast against the moss, and October–November foliage on the surrounding hillside turns the whole scene into a red-and-green tableau. Renge azaleas also bloom near the entrance in late spring.

## Getting There

There's no train or bus service to the park itself — a car is effectively required, roughly 40 minutes from JR Naganohara-Kusatsuguchi Station or 30 minutes from Kusatsu Onsen, with free parking at the visitor center. From there, a free shuttle bus covers the walk to Anajigoku in about 5 minutes, or visitors can take the marked 30-minute trail on foot.
